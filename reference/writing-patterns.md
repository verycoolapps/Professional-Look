# WRITING PATTERNS
## Named Structural Templates for Professional Communication

**Module:** `reference/writing-patterns.md`
**Loaded by:** Writing and rewriting tasks requiring structural guidance
**Dependencies:** `engines/writing-engine.md`, `engines/rewriting-engine.md`

---

## PURPOSE

Writing patterns are reusable structures — proven scaffoldings for different communication objectives. They are starting points, not constraints. Use the pattern to organize the thinking; then write the content so the structure becomes invisible.

---

## PATTERN 1: THE EXECUTIVE BRIEF

**Purpose:** Decision-oriented communication for people with limited time
**Used for:** Board communications, leadership emails, strategic memos, executive updates
**Tone profiles:** Executive, CEO/Founder, Corporate

### Template
```
SITUATION: [Current state in one sentence — data preferred]
COMPLICATION: [What has changed or what requires attention]
QUESTION: [The specific decision or response needed]
ANSWER: [The recommendation or proposed action]
NEXT STEPS: [Who does what by when — maximum three items]
```

### Example
```
SITUATION: Q3 revenue landed at $4.2M — 94% of plan, 28% year-over-year growth.
COMPLICATION: The SMB segment missed by 22%; enterprise exceeded by 14%. The mix shift is accelerating.
QUESTION: Should we reallocate the Q4 SMB marketing budget to enterprise expansion?
ANSWER: Yes — the unit economics and growth trajectory strongly favor enterprise focus through Q2 next year.
NEXT STEPS:
  · Finance model revised by Oct 15 (Sarah Chen)
  · Sales plan adjusted by Oct 20 (Revenue team)
  · Board notification by Oct 22 (CEO)
```

### When this pattern fails
When the audience wants narrative context before the recommendation. The Executive Brief assumes the audience is time-constrained and decision-ready. If context needs to be built first, use the Authority Stack or Story Arc instead.

## PATTERN 2: THE INSIGHT-FIRST

**Purpose:** Authority-building content that leads with the conclusion
**Used for:** LinkedIn posts, X threads, articles, thought leadership content
**Tone profiles:** Intellectual/Thought Leader, Executive, CEO/Founder

### Template
```
[Core insight / Conclusion / Counter-conventional claim — 1–2 sentences]

[Evidence 1: The specific experience, data, or observation that supports it]

[Evidence 2: Additional support — or the mechanism that explains why this is true]

[Implication: What this means for the reader or the field]
```

### Example
```
Most product roadmaps are not strategic documents. They are political negotiations frozen in time.

I've reviewed roadmaps at forty-three companies. The pattern holds across size and industry: features added because a board member asked, not because data confirmed demand. Timelines set to satisfy investors, not engineering reality.

The problem is not that companies lack good product thinkers. It is that the roadmap process aggregates competing interests rather than resolving them.

The consequence: everyone approved the roadmap and no one is accountable for the outcome.
```

### When this pattern fails
When the audience needs context before they will accept the conclusion. If the claim is very counter-intuitive, you may need to establish credibility first — use the Authority Stack instead.

## PATTERN 3: THE STORY ARC

**Purpose:** Trust-building content that conveys experience through narrative
**Used for:** LinkedIn posts, personal brand content, speeches, keynote content, sales narratives
**Tone profiles:** Warm Professional, Leadership, CEO/Founder

### Template
```
[Before: Specific situation — where things stood, what you believed, or what the problem was]

[Trigger: The event, decision, conversation, or realization that changed things]

[After: What happened — the specific outcome, not just "everything changed"]

[Lesson: The insight that came from the experience — your genuine, specific takeaway]
```

### Example
```
In 2019, I was six months into my first VP role and convinced the problem was my team.

The turnaround rate was high, output was inconsistent, and I had started keeping a private list of people I planned to move on from. I was managing the problem I could measure.

Then a colleague asked me one question: "What does your team need from you that you are not giving them?" I spent three days finding out.

The problem was not my team. It was that I had never been clear about what I expected.

Clarity is not kindness competing with performance standards. It is the minimum requirement for both.
```

### When this pattern fails
When the audience does not know or care about the author enough for a personal story to land. For newer audiences, lead with the lesson and use the story as supporting evidence rather than the frame.

## PATTERN 4: THE AUTHORITY STACK

**Purpose:** Establishing credibility on a specific claim through layered evidence
**Used for:** Content on contested claims, new audiences, expertise demonstration
**Tone profiles:** Intellectual/Thought Leader, Executive, Warm Professional

### Template
```
[Bold claim — stated directly, without hedge]

[Proof 1: First specific piece of evidence — experience, data, or named example]

[Proof 2: Second specific piece of evidence — ideally from a different domain or type]

[Broader truth: The principle that these proofs point toward]
```

### Example
```
Cold outreach email response rates are almost entirely determined by the first sentence.

We ran a controlled test across 2,400 emails: same offer, same recipient profile, different opening lines. The difference between a 3% response rate and a 21% response rate came down to whether the first sentence was about us or about them.

I've seen the same pattern hold in face-to-face sales: the rep who opens with a relevant insight about the prospect's business earns the conversation; the rep who opens with a company overview does not.

The mechanism: people respond to being understood, not to being sold. The first sentence is your only opportunity to demonstrate that before they have decided to move on.
```

### When this pattern fails
When the proofs are weak or unverifiable. An Authority Stack with thin evidence produces the opposite of authority. If the evidence is not available, use the Story Arc (personal experience) or the Insight-First (stated as personal perspective) instead.

## PATTERN 5: THE MAGNETIC HOOK

**Purpose:** First-line or first-paragraph that earns the read
**Used for:** All content types — this is a pattern for the opening, not the full piece
**Works with:** All other patterns as the opening element

### Hook type 1: The Contradiction
```
[Widely held belief] + [The evidence that complicates it]

Example: "Everyone talks about company culture. Almost no one measures it."
```

### Hook type 2: The Surprising Fact
```
[A specific, true, unexpected number or finding]

Example: "Seventy percent of leadership development spend produces zero measurable behavioral change."
```

### Hook type 3: The Bold Opinion
```
[Your genuine, defensible position — stated as a position, not a fact]

Example: "The best career advice I ever received was to stop networking and start being worth knowing."
```

### Hook type 4: The Specific Scenario
```
[A concrete moment, starting in the middle of the action]

Example: "The audit report was 142 pages. My CEO wanted a summary by 3 PM. I had six hours."
```

### Hook type 5: The Direct Question (earned)
```
[A question your ideal reader would pay to know the answer to]

Example: "How do you make a case to cut the projects you still believe in?"
```

### When a hook fails
- When it overpromises what the content delivers
- When it is so generic it could open any post by anyone
- When it tries to generate emotion before establishing credibility

## PATTERN 6: THE THREAD SPINE

**Purpose:** Long-form content structured for sequential reading on X/Twitter or LinkedIn
**Used for:** X threads, LinkedIn document posts, carousels
**Tone profiles:** All — adapt the spine to the chosen profile

### Template
```
TWEET/SLIDE 1 (Hook): [The payoff of the entire thread, stated as a claim or promise]

TWEET/SLIDE 2 (Foundation): [The context or principle that everything else builds on]

TWEET/SLIDE 3–N (Connectors): [Each makes one specific point; each ends in a way that makes the next one necessary]

TWEET/SLIDE FINAL (Landing): [The synthesis — the full picture, stated in one to two sentences]
```

### Thread structure principles
- Each connector tweet must work as a standalone sentence
- The connection between tweets should be logical, not just sequential
- Do not summarize the previous tweet at the start of the next one
- The landing tweet should feel like an arrival, not a repetition

## PATTERN 7: THE ANNOUNCEMENT FRAME

**Purpose:** Communicating news clearly and compelling the audience to care
**Used for:** Product launches, organizational news, career milestones, partnership announcements
**Tone profiles:** Executive, CEO/Founder, Leadership

### Template
```
[What: The announcement, stated clearly and specifically]

[Why it matters: The significance for the audience — what changes because of this]

[What's next: The first concrete step, milestone, or invitation]

[Optional: Brief acknowledgment of what made this possible — team, partners, journey]
```

### Example
```
We are launching [Product Name] today.

For the past three years, our enterprise customers have managed [specific workflow] in spreadsheets and email threads. That ends today. [Product Name] automates the process, integrates with the five tools every enterprise already uses, and cuts average completion time by 70% in early testing.

Access starts now: [link]. We are onboarding enterprise clients in order of application.

This was a long build. The team that built it is exceptional.
```

## PATTERN 8: THE QUIET FLEX

**Purpose:** Sharing professional achievement without explicit self-promotion
**Used for:** Career milestones, professional recognition, project completion
**Tone profiles:** Warm Professional, Leadership, Executive

### The principle
The achievement becomes evidence for a larger point about the field, the industry, or the lesson learned. The author is the vehicle, not the subject.

### Template
```
[The insight or observation that the achievement supports]

[The context — what was being done and why, briefly]

[The outcome — named, specific, but not the subject of the post]

[The real point — what this means beyond "I did something good"]
```

### Example (vs. bad alternative)
❌ "Honored and grateful to announce I've been recognized as one of the Top 40 Under 40 in finance! So thankful for this incredible team and amazing journey! #blessed"

✅ "Recognition lists have always meant less to me than the conversations they start.

Two years ago, this firm operated in four markets with a team of eighteen. Today: twelve markets, sixty-two people, three product lines that did not exist.

That work put us on someone's list. What it actually means: we found a way to grow without losing what made us good at what we do.

That is harder than the list suggests."
