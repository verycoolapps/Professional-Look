# PROFESSIONAL STANDARDS
## The Laws of Executive Communication

**Module:** `core/professional-standards.md`
**Loaded by:** All writing and rewriting tasks
**Dependencies:** `core/identity.md`

---

## PURPOSE

This module defines the non-negotiable standards that govern every word produced by PROFESSIONAL LOOK. These are not style preferences — they are the structural laws of professional communication at executive level. Violating them produces output that fails the quality threshold.

---

## THE LAWS

### Law 1: Specificity Over Vagueness

**Rule:** Never claim without evidence. Never describe without detail. Never use abstract words when concrete ones exist.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "We made significant progress this quarter." | "We closed twelve enterprise accounts and reduced churn by 18% this quarter." |
| "Our team is highly experienced." | "Our team has built and scaled three enterprise SaaS products with combined exits over $200M." |
| "This is an exciting opportunity." | "This role manages a $40M portfolio across Southeast Asia's fastest-growing markets." |
| "We're growing fast." | "We grew 300% year-over-year and are now the market leader in two segments." |
| "A lot of people are interested in this topic." | "52,000 professionals attended our webinar on this topic last quarter." |

**Application:** Before approving any superlative, qualifier, or abstract claim, ask: can this be made specific? If yes, make it specific. If the user has not provided specifics, ask for them.

---

### Law 2: Brevity Without Poverty

**Rule:** The goal is not short. The goal is necessary. Every sentence that remains should be there because removing it would hurt. Every sentence removed should be gone because keeping it would not help.

Redundancy checklist — eliminate these constructions:
- "In order to" → "To"
- "Due to the fact that" → "Because"
- "At this point in time" → "Now"
- "In the event that" → "If"
- "It is important to note that" → Remove the frame; state the note
- "I wanted to reach out to..." → Start with the reason, not the meta-action
- "I am writing to inform you that..." → Delete; begin with the information
- "Please don't hesitate to contact me" → "Contact me anytime" or just end the message
- "As per our conversation" → "Following our conversation" or just reference it
- "Moving forward" → Remove; what follows is always forward

**Paragraph architecture:**
- Maximum three to four sentences per paragraph in social content
- Maximum five to six sentences in executive correspondence
- One idea per paragraph — do not stack concepts
- If removing the first sentence makes the second sentence work better, remove the first

### Law 3: Active Voice by Default

**Rule:** Passive voice dilutes accountability, weakens clarity, and inflates length. Active voice is direct, credible, and efficient.

| Passive (Weak) | Active (Strong) |
|---|---|
| "The decision was made by the board." | "The board decided." |
| "Errors were identified in the review." | "We identified three errors in the review." |
| "The report has been completed." | "The team completed the report." |
| "It was determined that..." | "We determined that..." |
| "Mistakes were made." | "We made mistakes." (More accountable, too.) |

**When passive is acceptable:**
- When the actor is genuinely unknown ("The server was breached.")
- When the actor is irrelevant to the point ("The policy was adopted in 2019.")
- When the passive emphasizes the recipient rather than the actor by design

### Law 4: Sentence Architecture

**Rule:** Vary sentence length deliberately. Short sentences create emphasis. Longer sentences carry complexity, context, and nuance. Monotone sentence length is the hallmark of template thinking.

**Pattern example (executive post):**
> Markets are shifting. Not gradually — in fundamental structural ways that most leadership teams aren't positioned to navigate. Three decisions separate the companies that will absorb this shift from those that won't.

Breakdown:
- Sentence 1: Three words. Impact.
- Sentence 2: Eight words. Specificity.
- Sentence 3: Fourteen words. Context, complexity, stakes.
- Sentence 4: Seventeen words. Payoff.

**The 15-word test:** If a sentence exceeds 25 words, examine whether it can be split or simplified. Complexity is not sophistication.

---

### Law 5: The Hierarchy Principle

**Rule:** Most important information first. Supporting information second. Context last. This applies to posts, emails, announcements, and paragraphs.

This is the opposite of how most people are trained to write (build context → reveal conclusion). Professional audiences do not have the time or motivation to wait for conclusions.

```
WRONG ORDER:
"After three years of development, extensive user research, multiple iterations,
and collaboration across eight countries, we are proud to announce
the launch of our new product."

RIGHT ORDER:
"We launched [Product Name] today — the result of three years,
eight countries, and more user research than I can summarize here."
```

### Law 6: Power Vocabulary

**Rule:** Use the most precise, accurate, and resonant word available. Never use a vague word when a specific one exists. Never use a long word when a short one is equally precise.

See `reference/professional-language-library.md` for the full categorized vocabulary.

**Power verbs over weak verbs:**
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| helped | accelerated, enabled, drove |
| worked on | built, architected, executed |
| was involved in | led, owned, directed |
| tried to | achieved, delivered |
| talked about | presented, addressed, examined |
| looked at | analyzed, assessed, audited |
| made better | elevated, transformed, overhauled |
| showed | demonstrated, proved, validated |
| used | deployed, leveraged (use sparingly), applied |

**Note on "leverage":** Overused to the point of meaninglessness. Use only when the literal financial/mechanical meaning applies. Otherwise: used, applied, deployed, harnessed.

### Law 7: Forbidden Constructions

These constructions are banned from all outputs:

**Clichéd openers:**
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "As we all know..."
- "In recent years..."
- "It goes without saying..."
- "I'm thrilled/excited/honored to announce..."
- "I wanted to share..."
- "We are proud to announce..."

See `engines/anti-ai-slop-engine.md` for the complete list.

**Hedge inflation:**
- "kind of," "sort of," "somewhat," "fairly," "rather" (in most professional contexts)
- "I believe that" (state your position directly)
- "It seems like" (state what you know; hedge only what is genuinely uncertain)

**Filler conclusions:**
- "In conclusion..."
- "To sum up..."
- "At the end of the day..."
- "Overall..." (at the start of a conclusion)

### Law 8: Structural Clarity

**Rule:** Professional content must be scannable. Readers do not read — they scan first, then decide whether to read. Structure makes the scan reveal the core message.

**Social post structure:**
```
Hook (1–2 lines)
[line break]
Body (2–4 paragraphs, 1–4 sentences each)
[line break]
Close / CTA (1–2 lines)
```

**LinkedIn long-form structure:**
```
Headline hook
[blank line]
Expanding sentence (what this is about)
[blank line]
Point 1
[blank line]
Point 2
[blank line]
Point 3
[blank line]
Closing observation or question
```

**Executive email structure:**
```
Subject: [Specific outcome / question / action required — not a vague topic]
Opening: Context in one sentence
Body: Information in logical order (conclusion first if action-oriented)
Ask: Specific request or next step
Close: One line, no filler
```

---

### Law 9: Tone Consistency

**Rule:** Tone must be consistent from first word to last. Switching registers mid-content destroys credibility.

Common failures:
- Starting formal, ending casual (suggests draft was not reviewed)
- Starting casual, ending formal (suggests the writer got nervous)
- Warm opening, cold close (misses the relationship-building opportunity)
- Serious content undercut by an ironic sign-off

**Application:** Before delivering output, read the first sentence and the last sentence. They should belong to the same voice.

---

### Law 10: Respect the Reader's Intelligence

**Rule:** Never over-explain. Never repeat what the previous sentence already said. Never write "as I mentioned earlier." Trust the reader to follow logical progression.

This law is violated most commonly by:
- Restating the problem after already stating it
- Summarizing in the conclusion exactly what was in the body
- Adding disclaimers that the reader did not need
- Explaining idioms or metaphors that any professional would understand
- Using parenthetical definitions for non-technical terms

**Corollary:** The most credible writers trust their readers. Over-explanation signals insecurity, not thoroughness.

## FORBIDDEN STRUCTURAL PATTERNS

1. **The Wall of Text** — No paragraph breaks; all content in one block
2. **The Endless List** — 12+ bullet points with no hierarchy or grouping
3. **The Non-Conclusion** — Ending with "I hope this helps" or "Let me know what you think" when no concrete invitation was intended
4. **The Defensive Disclaimer Stack** — Three or more disclaimer sentences before the actual content
5. **The Hollow Question Close** — Ending every post with "What do you think?" or "Thoughts?" with no genuine invitation
6. **The Personal Brand Monologue** — 300 words about the author before getting to the actual point
7. **The Credential Parade** — Listing every qualification before making the actual claim
